Introduction
Concept and Objectives of Integrated Planning 
The purpose of this document is to outline the processes and governance for Integrated Planning at Medco E&P Grissik Ltd (MEPG). Integrated Planning is a set of cross-functional processes designed to combine, sequence, and coordinate all activities across short-term (Operational), medium-term (Tactical), and long-term (Strategic) Horizons within the Medco Asset Plan1. The goal is to produce an approved and prioritized planning and scheduling process that maximizes stakeholder value by efficiently identifying critical activities, resolving conflicts, and managing opportunities. These processes are designed to minimize siloed thinking and promote a collaborative approach to planning.
The objectives of effective Integrated Planning are to: 
Enhance Health Safety and Environment (HSE) performance through proactive planning and the avoidance of unplanned activities.
Increase value to the Company by identifying critical activities, resolving potential conflicts between stakeholders, and capturing and prioritizing opportunities.
Transform data into actionable information that supports optimized decision-making.
Achieve performance improvements and cost savings through better coordination and utilization of both company and contract resources, focusing on high-value work.
Measure planning performance using metrics to identify opportunities for learning and continuous improvement.
Increase production by minimizing down period and realizing opportunities.
Improve communication within the business and with supporting functions through effective planning and scheduling processes.
Provide a common communication platform for all stakeholders.
Ensure workforce alignment, ensuring every member understands their role in achieving short, medium, and long-term business unit goals.
Assure project success by guaranteeing timely completion of contracts, delivery of materials, and availability of required resources prior to field execution.
Minimize non-productive labor time through effective planning, leading to manpower cost savings.

The process should be consistent across all functions and disciplines, providing a unified foundation for collaboration. It should clearly define long-term objectives and goals, while setting clear expectations for all stakeholders.

Scope
The processes outlined in this guideline apply to the Planning and Scheduling activities for MEPG Asset-operated PSCs, Exploration, and Development activities. This includes the BU Life/Asset Development Plan (ADP), 10-Year/Long-Term Plan (LTP), Medium/Tactical/24-Month Plan, Short-Term/Operational/30-90 Day Plan, and detailed operational-level planning for individual functions.


Planning System
Integrated Planning Philosophy
At MEPG, we employ a cross-functional Integrated Planning process to plan and schedule activities across operational, tactical, and strategic horizons. This approach ensures the development of resourced, prioritized, and approved plans and schedules.
Integrated Planning Strategyc
Organization. 
Adequate and competent planning resources will be allocated at both the Functional and Asset levels. Each planning horizon will be managed and owned by the corresponding level of Asset leadership.
Planning Goals and Objective.  
MEPG’s Assets, Functions, and relevant individuals will collaborate to establish aligned goals aimed at achieving the short-, medium-, and long-term Planning and Scheduling (P&S) objectives.
Integrated Planning and Scheduling.  
All activities, including contractors, will be planned, scheduled, and assessed in alignment with the Integrated Planning process.
Planning Integration.  
The Strategic, Tactical, and Operational horizons will be aligned to support goals at each level, ensuring a cohesive approach to planning.
Planning tools, visualization and communication.  
Planning processes and communication methods will be tailored to meet the specific needs of stakeholders, enhancing understanding and collaboration.
Continuous Improvement.  
Regular and consistent reviews of planning performance will be conducted to refine practices and maintain the relevancy of business processes.
Planning Process.  
Each planning horizon will adhere to a structured business process, which includes:
Integration of activities across different functions to ensure a unified approach.
Establishment of a baseline plan that accounts for key constraints, synergies, and potential conflicts.
Differentiation between activities requiring high certainty and strict control versus those allowing future flexibility.
Approval of integrated plans by the designated ownership level for each planning horizon.
Implementation of defined processes to facilitate feedback and alignment between planning horizons.
Regular review of planning performance to identify opportunities for continuous improvement.


Other than the common strategies applicable across all functions, specific functions may incorporate tailored practices for P&S. Examples include:
Operations
Shutdown Planning.  Shutdown activities will be coordinated with functional activities and aligned with the maintenance schedules of both internal and external stakeholders, including buyers.
Projects 
Portfolio Management and Operability Execution.  The portfolio management of facilities and wells—from the Pre-Development stage through to execution, including contractor activities—will adhere to the Integrated Planning process and comply with the Medco Project Excellence Process (MPEP)
Operability assurance process will support operations engagement in portfolio management, facilitating adequate input during project development. During execution, the Operations Readiness Review process will support operations teams in preparation for startup, ensuring seamless handover and project closeout phases.
Well Operations and Services
Procurement activities will commence during the strategic planning phase, incorporating contingencies for potential changes in the tactical period.
The Well Delivery and Drilling Plan (WDDP) processes will be utilized to establish and regularly review baseline plans, minimizing late-stage changes.
Commercial
Contractual events, rights, and obligations outlined in each sales agreement will be integrated across relevant functions to optimize, protect, and/or create value for the company.
Market drivers will be regularly assessed and incorporated into production planning to align  with market dynamics and maximize value.
Integrated Planning Work Process
The Integrated Planning Work Processes are summarized across various planning horizons, as depicted in Figure 2:
BU Life Plan / Asset Development Plan (ADP) 
This plan comprises a comprehensive set of documents that outline strategic alternatives and development scenarios for the Medco Asset. It includes, Scenario Planning, Long-Term Growth or Divestment Planning and End-of-Life strategies for assets. The ADP aligns all groups and departments with the Asset's strategic goals and identifies specific milestones necessary to achieve these objectives. It serves as a reflection of the Vision and Mission of MEPG.
10-Year Plan / Long-Term Planning (LTP)
This Level 1 milestone plan typically spans ten or more years and is aligned with the annual LTP process. Baselines and performance are monitored and assessed against the Asset's annual goals.

24-Month Plan
The 10-Year Asset Plan is further detailed to Level 2 by Asset Management, Subsurface, and other relevant functions into 24-Month Plans. These plans are the primary focus of Integrated Planning, summarizing activities between 90 days and 24 months.
As needed, the 24-Month Plans may extend to cover the full scope of major programs, including: key milestones, major activities, commercial and contractual requirements, rig moves, major procurement tenders, engine overhaul programs and shutdown schedules. Both the 10-Year and 24-Month Plans are developed in parallel with the annual LTP budget cycle.
30/90-Day Plan
For field operational activities, the 30/90-Day Plan includes the fixed 30-Day activities and a 60-Day lookahead. For non-operational functions (e.g., project FEL, drilling FEL and etc.), the operational-level plan consists of detailed activity schedules required to execute day-to-day work. All stakeholders in operational planning are expected to provide timely updates on changes or progress and participate in monthly planning meetings.
Plan Control
Plan control involves a set of activities and processes designed to manage and monitor the effectiveness of plans. For field operations, these processes are detailed within a 7-D process, ensuring structured oversight and execution. Other functions utilize comparable processes tailored to their specific requirements, such as MPEP requirements or other relevant methodologies, to effectively manage day-to-day activities
The planning process is structured to follow a cyclical and iterative approach throughout the year, ensuring alignment with the LTP / Budgeting cycle. It encompasses all critical functions within MEPG, including Operations, Projects, and Drilling, as well as supporting functions.
The Maintenance Planning process is integrated into the broader Integrated Planning framework. It adheres to established Maintenance and Reliabilty practices, encompassing work order preparation, equipment procurement and delivery, assignment of sufficient resources and consideration of operational constraints.
Shutdown Planning is an integral component of the Integrated Planning framework.
Project Planning is also incorporated within Integrated Planning, with its functional execution governed by corporate standards outlined in the MPEP, document MPEP-PM-GUI-03. 
Early Operations involvement during the project lifecycle is crucial to ensure high reliability and operability of equipment. The planning process for achieving this level of performance is guided by the Operability Assurance (OA) Guideline, detailed in Document No. MPEP-PD-GUI-01.

Plan Ownership and Approval
Each plan, program, or logically grouped set of activities must have a designated Plan Owner who is accountable for its execution. In an asset-based organization, ownership of major capital program planning typically resides with the Asset Management teams. However, Plan Owners may also include Operations Managers, Subsurface Managers, or similar roles, depending on the scope and nature of the work. The responsibility for delivering the work and providing the necessary planning support to the Plan Owner lies with the supporting functions, such as Projects, Well Operations, Surface Engineering, Relations, and others.
In general, the Plan Owner is accountable for the following aspects of their respective projects or programs:
Planning Performance and Work Delivery,
Key premises and changes,
Conflicts resolution, issues and opportunities,
Milestone Status Updates,
Review of Long-Term Resource Status
Approval levels for each planning horizon are appropriately assigned to ensure organizational buy-in and visibility. Each horizon comprises multiple individual programs and projects, with accountability assigned to the respective Owners.
The Planning Process Focal Point oversees the management and implementation of business processes for each horizon, ensuring they align with the needs of the business unit. Continuous improvement updates to these processes are coordinated through the focal points to enhance efficiency and effectiveness.
Plan administrators are responsible for providing the integrated planning details required for each horizon and ensuring alignment with detailed cross-functional activity-level scheduling.

Governance Model
The governance model for integrated planning incorporates feedback loops to ensure that information and decisions flow effectively both top-down (from long-range to operational planning) and bottom-up. This model facilitates information sharing across planning horizons.
Meeting Term of Reference
Efficient and focused communication across the integrated planning processes requires that all regularly scheduled meetings be guided by a clear Terms of Reference (TOR). The TORs for each planning horizon are outlined in Appendix A – BU Life Planning and Appendix B – Meeting Term of Reference.


Criteria of Activities Inclusion into Plan
A typical list of activities for each planning horizon is summarized in Appendix C. These horizons generally align with the Numeric Schedule Levels as defined by s International Recommended Practice No. 37R-06, as follows:
10 Year plan: Corresponds to a Level 1 high-level schedule, focusing on key milestones and summary activities
24 Month Plan: Provides a breakdown of the Level 1 plan, highlighting the integration of major works across the lifecycle of a project or activity.
30/90D Plan: Represents the first level of activity-based planning, enabling critical path analysis and equivalent to a Level 3 plan.
Plan Control:  Comprises Level 4 and 5 plans, which detail the production of work packages at the deliverable level. This level of detail is typically reserved for execution or physical operational activities.


Managing the Plans
Acceptance/Input Criteria 
Maintaining consistent acceptance and input criteria is crucial for ensuring the compatibility and coordination of activities from various sources into a unified integrated plan. This approach facilitates the identification of potential conflicts, such as skilled labor availability, rig constraints, and capital limitations, while also uncovering synergies and optimizing the sequencing of activities. Typical input criteria for each planning horizon are detailed in Appendix D.
30/90 Day Plan
The first 30 days of an approved plan, issued monthly, are considered "frozen." This means that all work activities within this period are fully defined, with all preparatory work, equipment, and resources confirmed to be available for execution within the specified timeframe. If specific gate requirements are not met 30 days before the planned work commencement date, the job will be removed from the plan.
Activities in the 30 days plan should typically include detail for the following: 
Activity number.
Activity description.
Main Work SAP or Cost Centre.
Task lead/ responsible person for field work.
Man-Hour Estimates per operation and per resource.
Operation Calendar (Day-Night shift, Day and Night, 24 hour etc.).
Required Start and End Date.
Optimistic, Expected and Pessimistic Duration.
Functional Location at Order Level or Operation Level.
Number of personnel to be mobilized to field for the task broken down by company name (COPI and contractors), including supplier and specialist support.
Number of expected field operations personnel support.
Contractor schedule is ready.
List of specific tools, equipment required from Operations (crane, scaffolds, boat, vehicles, etc), consumables and applicable locations lists.
Any other specific requirement or information that Operations or other departments need to know.
Criteria for other functions (non-field operations) may include factors such as required work manhours, availability of specialists, service contract readiness, defined start and end dates, and the anticipated duration of the activities.
Gate Review Criteria 
Sheltering losses resulting from inefficient planning is achieved by ensuring high-quality planning that supports thorough analysis and integration. Two key gate reviews are implemented:
Sufficiency of Information Review: This step ensures that adequate and accurate information is entered into the plan. If the information provided is insufficient, planners must obtain additional details from the job owner. Typical information required is outlined in Appendix D.
Readiness Gate Review: This review assesses whether an activity is prepared for integration into the execution phase. It applies specifically to activities scheduled within the 30- and 90-day planning horizons. Activities failing to meet readiness criteria will not proceed to execution.
Gate Requirement for 90 Day plan
The following are the gate requirement for the activities to be included in the 90 Day Operations plan:
Funding in the Medco LTP and annual WP&B.
Obtain consent to proceed from Partners and internal Medco.
Obtain permitting requirement.
Engineering design sufficiently matured.
Execution contract is available.
Materials readiness.
Simultaneous Operations to avoid site conflict.
HSE Mitigation in place.
Gate Requirement for 30 Day plan
The following are the gate requirements for activities included in the 30-Day Operations Plan:
all necessary materials and tools have been delivered to the site and confirmed to meet the required specifications,
the work pack, including procedures, drawings, and other documentation, is complete and has been reviewed or approved,
internal support resources have been confirmed and meet all HSE requirements for mobilization,
the specialist vendor has been contacted and has provided a commitment, including personnel details, schedule, and mobilization date,
materials and positions required for stakeholder and government engagement have been confirmed and agreed upon.
Managing Constraints
When developing plans, especially at the 30/90-Day Plan level, several constraints must be considered. These constraints are typically classified into two categories: static and dynamic. Static Constraints tend to remain relatively unchanged over time and include factors such as the number of beds, life boat seat availability, regulatory permits, personnel access to specific areas, ongoing SIMOPS (Simultaneous Operations) restrictions, and limitations set by design or building codes.
Dynamic Constraints are subject to frequent changes and require a more responsive management process. Examples include weather conditions, seasonal access, availability of specialist services and personnel, and dynamic SIMOPS conditions (e.g., ongoing work, equipment downtime, etc.).
When developing plans, it is essential that all identified constraints are understood, communicated, and updated regularly. The following are typical constraints that should be considered as part of the P&S process.
Permitting and Other Stakeholders Approvals
The readiness of a work activity should consider all necessary clearances, permits, and approvals from relevant stakeholders. This includes budget and AFE approvals from government/SKKMigas, Partners, and internal Medco, as well as permits from agencies such as MIGAS, Seacomm, Aircomm, and local governments.
Resources Planning & Manpower Histogram
Resources, including headcount and associated costs, are tied to planned activities. Headcount resources should be validated and leveled according to numbers, competencies, and timing (e.g., manpower histogram). Hiring plans should also be considered as a potential constraint to ensure that the required skills are available when needed.
Cost constraints should be validated against the budgeted items in the LTP and the approved AFEs.
Personnel on Board and Bedding Capacity
One of the primary constraints to consider when preparing the 30/90-Day Field Plan is the Personnel on Board (POB) capacity at field locations. The total manpower should not exceed the pre-defined maximum POB.
Crew Change Schedule & Logistics
Field Asset Operations provide regular transportation for personnel mobilization and demobilization. Mobilization outside of these scheduled times may require additional approvals, such as from the area Superintendent.
Material transportation from Jakarta to the field is arranged regularly by the Material Management group. Contractors should also consider transportation arrangements for goods and materials when developing the plan.
General Contractors Capacity
The availability of 3rd party resources should be confirmed before they are included in the 30/90-Day Plan. The contractor's schedule must reflect their readiness to execute the work.
Material Readiness and Supply Chain 
Materials and contracts required for any task should be identified early, considering the procurement/contracting process, delivery times, and transportation to the field. Task leads are responsible for identifying the required materials, quantities, and specifications, and passing this information to the planners to create requisitions in the system if the materials are not available in stock.
If materials are available in stock, maintenance planners should make reservations and communicate with the Materials Management team to ensure the materials are available when needed at the site.
Planners will facilitate communication with Procurement and Materials Management through the contract administrator based in the Jakarta Office to expedite the procurement process, materials delivery, and report on materials readiness status.
Activities included in the 90-Day Plan must have confirmed materials delivery prior to the scheduled execution. Activities listed in the 30-Day Plan should have all materials delivered to the site or be en route. Any exceptions require approval from the OIM or Field Manager.
Work Pack 
The 90-Day Plan includes all activities that meet the 'entry criteria' within the 90-day window. Activities listed on the 90-Day Plan (but not yet on the 30-Day Plan) have at least 30 days to develop the work pack before being included in the 30-Day Plan.
Operations and Maintenance Activities
BPM identifies all plant and equipment that require maintenance across all assets and specifies when and by whom the work will be carried out. This includes Planned Maintenance Routines (PM), which are recurring activities performed at a set frequency, and Corrective Maintenance Routines (CM), which are created to address additional work arising from inspections, breakdowns, or repairs to plant and equipment.
Although BPM identifies when Preventive Maintenance activities are due, certain activities may need to be included in the Integrated Plan to raise awareness among the maintenance crew and field supervisors, ensuring alignment and optimizing resources. 
Risk Assessment
The characterization of risks associated with planned activities can facilitate prioritization. Risks should be evaluated in terms of potential impacts on safety, the environment, execution, production, and other relevant areas. Risk assessment and communication should be integrated throughout the planning process, with particular emphasis at the 30/90 Day field execution planning stage.
Strategic Planning Horizon
At the strategic level, risk assessments will be conducted based on their potential long-term impact on MEPG. Business prioritization at this level may encompass safety, environmental impact, production, and high-level SIMOPS activities. Specifically, completion risk for Major Projects can be characterized by developing risked schedules using a probabilistic Contingency Breakdown Report (CBR) methodology. This methodology may include calculating P10, P50, and P90 schedule and cost estimates.
Tactical Planning Horizon
At the Tactical Level, risk evaluations, similar to those at the Strategic Horizon, will address high-level risks but with a focus on individual program or asset risks. For instance, key risks to Major Projects will be identified in terms of both cost and schedule impacts. For operating assets, potential production downtime due to shutdown windows will be considered, and significant upcoming maintenance programs will be highlighted.
Operational Planning Horizon
At the operational level, detailed risk assessments are conducted for planned activities, considering the inherent hazards of each activity, project, or operation. 
During the design and planning stages, these assessments include analyses such as Hazard Identification (HAZID), Hazard and Operability (HAZOP) studies, SIMOPS analysis, Safety Integrity Level (SIL) evaluations, Quantitative Risk Assessment (QRA)/CBR, and other design risk assessments. As activities transition to field execution, the focus shifts to safety and execution readiness, using tools such as Pre-Job Hazard Analyses (PJHA) and toolbox talks. The MEPG risk matrix (Appendix E) may be used as applicable.
Contingency Planning
The outcomes of risk assessments will guide the evaluation, development, and planning of contingency measures. Key features of contingency management are as follows:
Contingency plans are developed by cross-functional teams representing relevant areas of expertise.
The allocation of resources and the approval process are commensurate with the assessed level of risk.
Contingencies for tasks and equipment, including necessary materials, resources, and logistics, are integrated into the project plan and schedule.
Schedule contingencies incorporate a defined recovery strategy.
Contingency planning may utilize a formalized methodology, such as the QRA/CBR employed by Projects, or be addressed as a standard component of regular planning meetings.
Conflict Resolution and Plan Prioritization
When activities are added to the integrated plan, potential conflicts may arise at various stages. Prioritization of activities should be guided by standard criteria, with the primary objectives being:
Ensuring adherence to safety and environmental standards.
Maximizing value creation for the BU.
Maintaining alignment with the goals and objectives of the BU.
At different planning horizons, prioritization may take various forms:
Strategic Planning Horizon
Prioritization is typically based on Net Present Value (NPV) or risk assessments, with a focus on alignment with BU goals and objectives.
Tactical Planning Horizon
Similar to the Strategic Horizon, prioritization in the Tactical Horizon also considers NPV and risk but is more specifically tailored to individual projects, programs, or activities
Operational Planning Horizon
At the Operational Horizon, prioritization and conflict resolution are centered on addressing immediate risks. Recommended criteria for prioritizing activities and resolving conflicts include, but are not limited to:
Health, Safety, Environment
Asset Integrity
Production Loss
Human Factor and Working Environment (i.e. noise, morale, physical conditions, etc.)
Execution Opportunity Costs / Synergy Savings
Adequate planning / preparation time and resource allocation
Consideration of fatigue and overall activity impact.

For Operational Horizon, each function planner is responsible for maintaining and storing detailed planning files, while Tactical and Strategic Horizons, summary plans are compiled and stored in a centralized SharePoint repository to ensure accessibility for a broader audience.

Effective data management enables asset leadership and data management personnel to define the requirements for data access and organization. Plan Communication and Visualization
The plans should be communicated effectively to relevant employees and contractors to ensure that all individuals clearly understand the objectives and their personal contributions toward achieving the planned goals. Visualization of the plans at each horizon should enable a quick and comprehensive understanding of the schedules, milestones, and overall strategy.
Change Control for Operational Planning Horizon
Changes to the approved 30-day plan should be minimized. However, if a change is deemed necessary, it must follow the appropriate authorization process.
For field operation activities, changes that do not affect constraints (e.g., bed capacity, SIMOPS, cost, HSE risks, or other issues) can be approved by the Plan Owner. If the proposed change impacts constraints, it must be escalated to the OIM for review, with final approval at the discretion of the Field Manager (FM).
When evaluating changes that impact constraints, the FM should consider factors such as the criticality of the work, resource availability, logistics, POB capacity, and other relevant considerations.
A ‘change’ to the approved 30-Day Plan is defined as any of the following:
Adding work to the approved plan, particularly if it requires additional resources or support. Depending on the nature and criticality of the work, email confirmation from the FM or OIM may suffice.
Delays to tasks within the approved plan that could impact constraints or other tasks in the plan.
Inclusion of projects or activities that require additional budget.
Other additions, cancellations, or delays deemed substantial enough to require authorization.
When a change to the 30-Day Plan is necessary, the Plan Change Control Procedure outlined in Appendix F should be utilized.

Assessing Performance
Debriefing and After Action Reviews (AAR)
The AAR is a valuable tool for learning from both successes and failures. Its primary objective is to engage participants involved in the activity to review variances from the plan. This collaborative approach allows leaders to leverage group dynamics and reach a shared understanding of key lessons learned.
The AAR should focus on identifying lessons that can significantly enhance performance. These insights, whether they are best practices or opportunities for improvement, should be integrated into the overall strategy to drive continual progress.
The benefits of the AAR approach:
Provides valuable insights for others involved in similar activities, helping to avoid repetitive mistakes and unnecessary relearning.
Facilitates a common understanding of actual events and variances from the original plan.
Identifies opportunities for improvement and establishes actionable steps for progress.
Ultimately aims to improve the way we conduct our business and achieve better outcomes.
Further details about the AAR, including its processes, structure, and application, can be found in Appendix G – Conducting After Action Review.
Planning Compliance 
Assessing compliance with the BU planning processes requires multilateral communication between planners, plan users, and plan owners. Compliance is not just about whether processes are being followed, but also about their understanding, value, and their role in facilitating continuous improvement. If processes are not being adhered to, it may indicate that the processes themselves need to be rectified.
Peer Review
Peer reviews enable independent observers to evaluate planned activities and identify potential flaws and opportunities for improvement.
Documentation pertaining to field operations peer reviews is available on the respective asset's Field Operations Planning site. In general, peer reviews should be conducted for field operations involving:
Shutdown plans that cause a reduction of at least 50% in the normal production rate.
Activities classified as having high or significant safety risks.
For projects executed under Corporate Authorization, peer reviews are an integral component of the MPEP and are documented within the corresponding project records.
Business Performance Review 
Robust management systems are designed to drive continuous improvement in organizational effectiveness and efficiency. Three fundamental components contribute significantly to sustained business performance improvement:

Governance: The framework that governs and sustains business performance reviews over time.
Documentation: The systematic collection of records that define the underlying data, analyses, and visualizations.
Metrics:  The established standards used to measure business performance. Performance reviews for P&S typically consider the following metrics:
Performance in terms of milestones, production, and costs across various timeframes (e.g., plan-on-plan, gate-on-gate, year-on-year).
KPIs, including man-hour, cost, and schedule efficiency, and plan attainment.
Clear accountabilities for plan ownership, review, and business performance have been established within the business processes, with a focus on the Tactical and Strategic Horizons.
Key Performance Indicator (KPI)
KPIs are metrics that are visible across the organization and are used to highlight and drive performance within P&S. These key metrics are documented in the business process maps associated with each horizon and in the overall Management Control and Reporting System (MCRS).
At the Operational Horizon, which focuses on the short term, metrics related to attainment, stability, and efficiency are applied. In contrast, longer-term KPIs primarily assess compliance with the planning process. Certain functions, such as Projects, maintain supplementary statistics for internal purposes, including man-hour, cost, and schedule efficiency; however, these are not comprehensively detailed within the BU procedure.
The following secondary performance measures may be considered for further development into defined KPIs:
Plan Utilization: This metric measures the extent to which planned activities on baselined work align with the resources available for their completion.
Plan Stability: This metric quantifies the number of interventions or changes made to the approved plan during the defined planning period.
Work Efficiency: This metric compares the amount of planned work performed (both scheduled and unscheduled) to the resources available for performing that work.
Disturbance/Delay: This metric tracks non-productive time associated with activity execution, categorized using standardized reporting codes, such as delays due to material availability, weather conditions, or permit approvals.

Future State – Dynamic Plan Management
The ideal future state of planning is one where decision-makers have enhanced visibility into their business and are empowered to make informed, efficient decisions. This vision moves beyond traditional “top-down” or “bottom-up” planning processes, fostering greater interaction between decision-makers and the analysts who provide critical data.
Dynamic Planning enables decision-makers to pose questions about the plan without the need for repeating, resubmitting, or redoing work. Adjustments to sensitivities, such as price, foreign exchange, or inflation, can be applied seamlessly without requiring involvement from the entire organization.
Integrated Planning centralizes planning data by aligning budget, economic, and financial planning processes. By eliminating conflicting data sources, this approach ensures that the resulting information and analyses are accessible at all organizational levels. The outcome is a more efficient and well-informed organization that can respond effectively to changing circumstances.

Appendix F – Activity Change Request Process

Reason for Plan Change
Changes to the 30-day plan must be approved by the Plan Owner due to the potential risks associated with adding or modifying activities. Such changes, if not carefully managed, could lead to Health, Safety, and Environmental (HSE) incidents, failure to meet forecasted deadlines, or negative impacts on overall plan performance.
There are three types of change to the plan:
Addition of New Activities
Deletion of Existing Activities
Modifications to Ongoing Activities within the 30-Day Plan
For operations activities, all proposed changes must be reviewed and approved during the daily or weekly meetings with the Superintendent, OIM, and/or Offsite Managers. These changes must also be documented through email confirmation or meeting minutes. Alignment with the existing schedule and required resources must be addressed during the meeting.
Any proposed changes that may impact constraints—such as safety, operational resources, logistics support, production, or budget—must be initiated through a Plan Change Request. At a minimum, such changes require approval from the Plan Owner.
New Activity to Plan
This process ensures that all unplanned activities with potential impacts on constraints (such as accommodations, operational resources, logistics support), production, or budget are clearly defined and agreed upon before being incorporated into the ongoing 30-day plans.
Changes with potential impacts on constraints—such as safety, operational resources, logistics support, production, or budget—require an approved Plan Change Request (PCR) form, with the following process:
Submission of PCR Form:
The Activity Sponsor completes and submits the PCR form to the Asset Planner for evaluation.
If there are no significant constraints (e.g., accommodations or SIMOPS issues), the changes can be forwarded directly to the Plan Owner for approval.
If there is a major impact on constraints, the matter must be escalated to the OIM for resolution. Depending on the complexity of the change, the OIM may convene a meeting with the Field Manager to discuss and resolve the issue.
The Field Manager reviews the PCR form, considering the priority, justification, and potential impacts on the current plan. Based on this review, the Field Manager decides whether to approve or reject the proposed activity.
The Asset Planner informs the Activity Sponsor whether the proposed activity is approved or rejected, including the reasons for the decision. If the change impacts other activities, the owners of those activities must be informed of the implications, and adjustments or reprioritization may be required.
Deletion of Activities
The deletion of an activity can be decided by the OIM after evaluating the following factors:
Impact on Budget and Production Opportunity: The potential consequences of continuing the activity versus its removal, including financial and production considerations.
Resource Reallocation: The reallocation of resources freed by the deletion and how it affects other operations.
Impact on Other Activities: Any effects on other activities that depend on or follow sequentially from the deleted activity.
Approval for deleting an activity that may impact constraints or resources must be documented through a PCR. The OIM is responsible for providing justification based on the considerations outlined above, while the Asset Planner is responsible for recording and documenting the justification.

Changes within ongoing 30 day planning
Any change to a plan that extends beyond three days from its original 30-day schedule requires alignment with the existing schedule and available resources. If the change impacts other activities, the respective activity owners must be informed, as they may need to re-prioritize their work accordingly.

Appendix G – Conducting After Action Review

Planning the AAR
Identify the activity to be reviewed.
Decide on the timing of the AAR – it should be conducted as soon as possible after the event.
Coordinate with the leader to determine who needs to attend.
Ensure a suitable location or room is available.
The leader should encourage the organization to capture key lessons in preparation for the AAR

Preparing the AAR
Select a facilitator.
Confirm the date, time, location, and duration of the session in advance.
Set expectations for attendees to come prepared and ready to focus on key issues

Conducting the AAR
The AAR should be focused, facilitated, and last no longer than 60 minutes.
Typically, the AAR will involve input from multiple perspectives and will not be conducted in isolation.
Promote active participation through group discussion.
Facilitate discussions in chronological order of events.
Record key lessons on a flip chart or similar tool.
Clearly define and agree on action items, including the responsible parties, specific tasks, and deadlines.
Highlight the potential impact of each lesson where known.

Follow up the AAR
Ensure documented lessons are tracked to closure, with their added value quantified. Use an Action Register to monitor progress.
Provide the leader with access to the AAR for discussion during lessons learned meetings.
